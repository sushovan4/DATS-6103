Association Analysis


Reading for this part: 26 min

“It is a capital mistake to theorise before one has data.”
– Arthur Conan Doyle

Everything since October has been supervised: there was an outcome, and the job was to predict it. This week there is no outcome. You have a pile of transactions—baskets, click sequences, diagnoses per patient, error codes per server—and the question is what goes with what.

That question has a combinatorial problem at its heart. With only 6 distinct items there are 63 possible itemsets to check; with 100 items there are more than 10^{30}. Association analysis is the set of ideas that make the search tractable, and then the set of measures that stop you believing the results.

The second half matters more than students expect. The algorithm will happily hand you a rule with 75% confidence describing a relationship that is negative, and telling the difference is one subtraction that almost nobody performs.

What is here

Itemsets and Support

Transactions, itemsets, and support. Why the obvious algorithm is impossible, stated as a number rather than as a worry.

The Apriori Algorithm

One property—every subset of a frequent itemset is frequent—and the pruning it licenses. On the worked example it examines 22 candidates where brute force would examine 63.

Rules, Confidence and Lift

Turning itemsets into rules, and the measure that saves you: milk → nappies has 75% confidence and a lift of 0.94, which means buying milk makes nappies less likely.

Practice Problems

Including a rule that looks excellent and is an artifact of one popular item.

Where this sits

It is the first genuinely unsupervised method in the course and the last before clustering. Both answer what structure is in this data rather than what is the value of y, and both are evaluated by whether the structure is useful rather than by an error rate—which is a different kind of judgment, and worth practicing.
