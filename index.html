Course Description

This course introduces the concepts and techniques of data mining as applied to data science, using Python throughout. Python Bootcamp is a prerequisite; the course opens with advanced language features and moves into linear algebra, Numpy, and Pandas. Through weekly labs and homework, students acquire, clean, and visualize real-world data, explore it, and model it with a range of data mining methods: regression, trees and ensembles, k-NN, association analysis, and clustering. The second half culminates in a team project applying those methods to a dataset of the team’s choosing.

Students are expected to use AI assistants and to identify when those assistants are wrong. Assignments are graded on that judgment as well as on correctness; see Use of AI Assistants.

As an introductory course, it covers a broad spectrum rather than a specialized area, and prepares students for the more advanced courses in the program.

Learning Outcomes

This course is built on the conviction that data science is best understood through the lens of matrices. The three weeks we spend on arrays and linear algebra in September are not a detour: they come back when least squares turns out to be a projection and the SVD turns out to explain what ridge regression shrinks, and again when clustering needs PCA before distances mean anything. As a result of completing this course, students will be able to:

  1. Write well-structured Python code, including basic object-oriented constructs, for common data science tasks;
  2. Represent data as vectors, matrices, and tensors in Numpy, reasoning fluently about shapes, axes, and broadcasting, and apply matrix decompositions (QR, SVD, eigendecomposition) both to concrete problems— solving linear systems, PCA, image compression—and to explaining what regression, regularization, and clustering are doing geometrically;
  3. Acquire, clean, reshape, and merge real-world data with Pandas, including principled handling of missing values;
  4. Produce and interpret visualizations with Matplotlib and Seaborn as part of a coherent exploratory data analysis;
  5. Build and tune supervised and unsupervised models—regression, logistic regression, trees, random forests and gradient boosting, k-NN, and k-means—and select evaluation metrics appropriate to the task;
  6. Mine unlabelled data for structure: derive frequent itemsets and association rules, and judge them using support, confidence, and lift;
  7. Apply sound evaluation practice: separate training from testing, use cross-validation, reason about the bias–variance trade-off, and recognize overfitting and data leakage;
  8. Critically evaluate an analysis, whether their own, a peer’s, or one produced with the help of an AI assistant, and identify specifically where and why it goes wrong;
  9. Explain and defend an analysis out loud—the choice of model, its limitations, and what the result does and does not support—to both technical and general audiences.

Course Prerequisites

This course does not teach Python. Python Bootcamp is an enforced prerequisite, and from the second week onward we work in Numpy and Pandas at a pace that assumes the language itself is not what you are struggling with. The first session covers advanced constructs only—classes and objects—not the basics.

What you are expected to arrive knowing

You should be able to do all of the following without looking them up:

  • Write a function, including default and keyword arguments;
  • Use list and dictionary comprehensions;
  • Work with nested lists and dictionaries and index into them confidently;
  • Use for and while loops and if/elif/else branching;
  • Read a traceback, identify the failing line, and fix the error;
  • Install a package and import it.

If more than one or two of those give you pause, act in the first two weeks rather than the sixth.

Week 1 self-check

The Aug 25 lab includes a short ungraded Python diagnostic taken under lab conditions: no AI assistants, no notes. It does not count toward your grade. Its only purpose is to tell you, while you still have time to act, whether your Python is where this course assumes it is.

Take the result seriously. The in-class labs are 35% of your grade and are also unaided, so the diagnostic is an honest preview of them—and because AI assistants are permitted on homework, homework will not reveal a gap here until it is too late to fix. Students may drop through the end of the fourth week of classes without a W on the transcript; see the Registrar for the exact date.

Course Format

This course is taught as a flipped workshop. Lecture material is delivered as assigned reading; class time is spent on discussion and supervised lab work. Slides are not presented in class, and content is not re-covered from the front of the room. Reading before each session is therefore required.

Each session runs in three modes:

  • Question triage. Instructor-led, drawn entirely from the questions students posted on the reading.
  • Reasoning and discussion. Students predict program output, justify an answer at the board, and defend it to one another. Understanding why an operation behaves as it does is the object of the course; syntax is not.
  • Lab. Supervised hands-on work at the keyboard.

Before class: readings and questions

Each class day has an assigned reading, listed in the course schedule. Most are chapters of these course notes; the machine learning weeks use An Introduction to Statistical Learning.

Every chapter of the notes carries a comment box backed by GitHub Discussions. Students post questions on the specific chapter as they read, and may also answer one another. Week overview pages have no comment box. Students are expected to post at least one substantive question or answer per class day; this forms the participation component of the grade.

Questions are due Monday at 12:00 noon, ahead of Tuesday’s class.

The reading for the first class is a review of Python from the Bootcamp material and the catch-up resources above, rather than a chapter of these notes.

Week 1 is ungraded

No work on Aug 25 counts toward the final grade, including the lab, the diagnostic, and participation. Graded participation begins with the Sep 1 class; the first questions are due Monday, Aug 31 at 12:00 noon.

Labs

Labs are the main activity of every session and are 35% of the grade. The Aug 25 lab is ungraded; grading begins Sep 1.

Labs are assessed on observed participation rather than on submitted files. Each session is worth three points:

Component Criterion
Interview Took both roles in the Part A interview, confirmed by a signed rubric card from whoever interviewed you.
Discussion Contributed substantively at the checkpoint.
Build Engaged with Part B and able to describe the current difficulty when asked.

None of the three criteria requires a correct answer. Notebooks are uploaded to Blackboard as a record and may be consulted if a grade is questioned, but they are not what earns the points; a notebook submitted by a student who was not present earns nothing.

There is no class on Nov 17. That session is made up on Thursday, Dec 10, the university Make-Up/Reading Day.

Documentation is allowed; AI assistants are not. The official NumPy, Pandas, Matplotlib and scikit-learn documentation and these course notes may be consulted freely during labs. See Use of AI Assistants.

Together with the exams, labs form the unaided portion of the grade.

How we work in the lab

Students work in a pair for Part A and a group of three or four for Part B; groups change every few weeks. When an odd number is present, one Part A group is a trio and the interview goes round it—A interviews B, B interviews C, C interviews A—so everyone still takes both roles. Three roles rotate within each Part B group so that every student takes each one over the semester, and in a group of four two share the skeptic’s job:

  • The driver types. No one else touches the keyboard.
  • The navigator reads documentation and directs the work, and does not type.
  • The skeptic owns the self-check cells and is responsible for identifying what would falsify each result.

At the checkpoint the instructor puts a question to the room, allows a minute to confer, and calls on one student at random to answer for the group.

The two lowest lab scores are dropped. There are twelve graded labs, and the lab grade is the best ten. No explanation, prior permission, or documentation is required. Beyond those two, a missed lab receives a zero; labs are not made up outside class. Students facing a longer disruption should contact the instructor early.

Participation

Participation is 10% of the grade, earned by posting substantive questions or answers on the reading pages by Monday noon, beginning with the Sep 1 class, and by contributing in class. A substantive question demonstrates engagement with the material: “I followed the derivation until the transpose appeared, why is it there?” rather than “I don’t understand chapter 4.”

Participation is recorded at twelve sessions—every meeting except Aug 25, which is ungraded, Oct 27, which is Exam 1, and the presentation session. No points are recorded on those, nor in the weeks with no class: Oct 13, Nov 17 and Nov 24. As with labs, the two lowest weeks are dropped, which leaves ten sessions counting.

Use of AI Assistants

Generative AI tools are permitted in this course under the conditions below, and students are responsible for the correctness of anything they submit. Questions about a particular case should be raised before submission.

Assessment AI policy
Homework Encouraged, with disclosure (see below)
In-class labs Not permitted
Exam 1 Not permitted
Project Permitted, must be disclosed in the report

Homework: disclosure is part of the submission

Any AI assistant may be used on homework. Submissions must then include:

  1. The working code.
  2. A prompt log—the prompts given and the responses received, as a transcript or an exported share link.
  3. A critique of roughly 150–300 words identifying what the assistant got wrong, or what would have gone wrong had its output been accepted unchanged. The critique should be specific: the line, the incorrect function, the silent type coercion, the misreading of the question.

The critique is graded alongside the code. A submission with correct code and a thoughtless critique scores below one with imperfect code and a sharp critique.

Where no assistant was used, write “No AI used” in place of the prompt log and use the critique to identify the part of the solution the student is least confident in.

Labs and the exam: no AI

In-class labs and Exam 1 are unaided and together constitute 50% of the grade. Use of an assistant on either is an academic integrity violation under the GW Code.

Unaided does not mean closed-book: the official library documentation and these course notes may be consulted freely during labs.

Fabricating a prompt log

Submitting an invented or doctored transcript is falsification of data under the Academic Integrity Code and will be reported. If you did not use AI, say so—that is always an acceptable answer.

This policy differs from other sections

Other sections of DATS 6103 may set a different AI policy, and some prohibit what this one requires. You are bound by the policy of the section you are enrolled in, which is this one. If you are also taking DATS 6101 with me, the policies match.

Assignments

Assignments are individual and due weekly, sometimes bi-weekly. Each assignment is graded on correctness of the code and on the quality of the AI disclosure described above. Details are posted on Blackboard. Every assignment counts—no assignment score is dropped.

Exam

Exam 1 is held in class on Tuesday, October 27 and covers the material through Oct 20: Python, linear algebra and Numpy, Pandas, exploratory data analysis, and the foundations of statistical learning.

It sits a week earlier than the material would suggest because Nov 3 is Election Day. Polls in the District close at 8:00 p.m. and this class runs to 8:40, so an exam that evening would have asked a student who had not already voted to choose. Regression as prediction moves to Nov 3 and is therefore not on the exam.

The exam is individual and taken without AI assistance. As in labs, this does not mean from memory; permitted materials will be announced in advance.

The exam falls where it does deliberately. This course is a prerequisite for six others, including the capstone, and the material it covers is what those courses assume. It is the one point in the term where that foundation is checked unaided.

Project

The machine learning half of the course is assessed by a project rather than a second exam. Teams of two or three choose a dataset and a question, apply the methods from Nov 10 onward, and report what the analysis supports.

The project is proposed Nov 10 and presented in the university final examination period, December 11–17, at the time assigned by the Registrar. Proposing it early is intentional: the methods arrive week by week, and the project is meant to absorb them as they arrive rather than to begin after the last one.

Deliverables are a GitHub repository, a written report stating what the analysis does and does not support, an AI disclosure statement, and a presentation.

Presentation questions are answered individually, and members of one team may receive different grades. A project is worth more than an exam to a student who wants something to show an employer, but it is worth nothing as an assessment if one member can carry the rest. The individual defense is where that is settled.

Late and Missed Work

Assignments may be submitted up to 48 hours late, twice in the term, with no penalty and no reason required. Say in the submission that you are using one. Beyond those two, and beyond 48 hours, an assignment is not accepted and scores zero. Every assignment counts, which is why the two extensions exist: there is no dropped assignment score behind them.

Labs are done and handed in during class and cannot be late; the two dropped labs are the accommodation there, as the two dropped weeks are for participation. None of this needs documentation or prior permission.

Exam 1 and the project are fixed and cannot be dropped. A make-up is arranged only for a documented emergency, and only if you contact me as early as you are able.

Independent or Out-of-Class Learning

The average minimum amount of out-of-class or independent learning expected is 5 hours per week, for our 2.5 hours of classroom meetings. You are strongly encouraged to make use of office hours and other resources including those offered by library services. Do not wait until it is too late to get help.
