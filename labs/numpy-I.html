Lab 3

Predicting shapes and outputs before running them.

Work in the browser on this page, or download lab3-numpy-I.ipynb and work in Jupyter. Both hold the same problems and the same checks; in the browser the checks are hidden and mark your answer for you, and in the notebook they are written out as assertions you run yourself.

How this session works
Part A—Discussion Worked in pairs, away from the keyboard. Not submitted. This is where participation is earned.
Part B—Build Graded on what you do in the room, not on the file you upload.

Labs are worth 35\% of the grade. They are completed without AI assistance and with documentation open—the NumPy reference, your notes, the textbook. That split is the whole point of the course: function names are meant to be looked up, reasoning is not. Nothing in this lab can be answered by finding the right function name.

Every problem carries a timer and a Focus button. The suggested times sum to roughly the session’s lab time; use them to pace yourself, not to panic.

Before you start, you should have worked through Arithmetic & Indexing, Broadcasting and the practice problems in Practice Problems. This lab does not re-run them. It assumes them.

Part A—Discussion

Format: pair mock interview

Work in pairs. One of you plays the interviewer and holds the rubric card; the other answers out loud, without typing. Then swap. Do not read the rubric card when you are the one answering.

Holding the rubric is not the easy seat. Knowing what a good answer contains is a harder skill than producing one, and it is the skill that makes you useful in a code review. Take that half seriously.

Each question is labeled by role and stage—the kind of screen it belongs to—rather than by company. Attributions to named companies are usually wrong, stale, or under NDA, and a wrong attribution misleads whoever is preparing.

Two more notes on the framing. The five-minute clock on each question is part of the simulation: real interview answers are time-boxed, and an answer that arrives at minute nine is a different answer. And this is the honest reply to why can’t I use AI in labs, though the reply has changed. It used to be that no interview let you use one. As of this year some do: Google is piloting a coding round with an approved assistant, and about a quarter of employers in New York now permit it. What those interviews assess is whether you catch what the assistant got wrong—prompt, then validate, then debug—and you cannot do that for work you could not have done yourself. The lab is not simulating a workplace. It is simulating the gate you have to pass to reach one.

Nothing in Part A is submitted.

A1. Say the shape out loud

Pair-programming screen, data science generalist.

“I have a tensor T of shape (365, 25, 3)—days, respondents, features. I am going to read you five expressions. For each one, tell me the shape of the result and, in one sentence, why.”

T.mean(axis=1)
T.mean(axis=(0, 1))
T[:, 0]
T[:, 0:1]
T.mean(axis=1, keepdims=True)
Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer:

  • states the rule before working the examples: the axis you name is the axis that disappears;
  • separates the two mechanisms—a reduction removes an axis, an integer index removes an axis, a slice keeps it (possibly at length 1);
  • gets axis=(0, 1) as collapsing two axes at once, leaving (3,);
  • explains what keepdims=True is for, not just what it does: it preserves the axis so the result still lines up against the original under broadcasting.

Listen for this wrong answer: “axis=1 means it works along the rows, so you get the row values back”—reading the axis argument as the axis you keep. It gives the right answer for T[:, 0] by luck and the wrong one for every reduction.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “Now I want to subtract each day’s average respondent from every reading in that day. Which of those five results can I subtract from T directly, and which one raises?”

A2. The line that ran and was wrong

Take-home follow-up discussion, ML-leaning role.

“This line is from the take-home you submitted. X holds observations in rows and features in columns.

Z = X / X.std(axis=1)

It ran without error, and your write-up says the columns are standardized. Walk me through whether that is true.”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer:

  • treats it as a broadcasting question and right-aligns the shapes out loud: (m, n) against (m,), so the divisor lands on the last axis;
  • notices that it only runs at all when m = n, and that on a non-square array it would have raised—so the square test data was an unkindness, not a pass;
  • gives both corrections and says which the write-up claimed: X.std(axis=0) to scale columns, X.std(axis=1, keepdims=True) to scale rows;
  • names the general hazard: broadcasting is silent when it succeeds, so a shape bug on square data produces numbers rather than an exception.

Listen for this wrong answer: “std defaults to the wrong axis, so just drop the argument.” Dropping it reduces over the flattened array and gives one scalar—a third wrong answer that also runs.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “Your unit test used a 4\times4 matrix and passed. What shape should the test have used, and what would it have caught?”

A3. Views, copies, and the bug you cannot see

Phone screen, data-engineering-leaning role.

“In NumPy, when does indexing hand you a view and when does it hand you a copy? And why should I care which one I got?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer:

  • draws the line correctly: basic slicing, reshape, .T, swapaxes, np.newaxis give views; integer-array (fancy) indexing, boolean masks, .copy(), .astype() give copies;
  • explains why a boolean mask must copy—the selected positions need not form a rectangular block and their number is not known until run time, so no shape-and-strides description of them exists;
  • says why views exist at all: slicing a 10 GB array costs nothing because nothing is duplicated. Views are the reason NumPy is fast, not a wart;
  • names the way to check rather than guess: np.shares_memory(a, b), or b.base.

Listen for this wrong answer: “slicing copies, the same as a Python list.” It is the single most common source of silent data corruption in NumPy code, precisely because the syntax is identical to the list case.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “You ship a helper that clips outliers. A caller reports that their original data changed underneath them. What did you write, and what is the one-line fix?”

A4. Which one would you ship?

Onsite, ML engineer.

“Two implementations of the same idea:

def scale_a(x):
    x = x / x.max()
    return x

def scale_b(x):
    x /= x.max()
    return x

Which of these changes the caller’s array? And which one would you put in a shared library?”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer:

  • states the distinction in general terms, not just for this snippet: name = value rebinds a name; name[...] = value and the augmented assignments mutate the object. scale_a rebinds a local and leaves the caller untouched; scale_b writes through;
  • notices that scale_b also returns the array, so a caller who writes y = scale_b(x) now has two names for one array and no warning about it;
  • takes a position on the API question and defends it—either “return fresh, and offer an explicit inplace= or out= for the callers who need it”, or “mutate, and say so in the name”—and points at how NumPy and pandas resolve the same tension;
  • mentions defensive copying at the top of the function as the cheap fix when in doubt.

Listen for this wrong answer: “they are the same; /= is just shorthand for x = x / ...”. It is not, and the difference is observable from outside the function.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “The caller passes np.arange(10). Now what happens?” (Integer array, in-place true divide: scale_b raises a casting error while scale_a quietly returns floats. Same call, two different failure modes.)

A5. Read the traceback

Debugging screen, any data role.

“Three tracebacks, no code, no running anything. For each one: what did the author most likely write, and what is the fix?

``` ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together with shapes (365,25,3) (365,3)

ValueError: The truth value of an array with more than one element is ambiguous. Use a.any() or a.all()

AxisError: axis 2 is out of bounds for array of dimension 2 ```”

Rubric card—interviewer only

A good answer:

  • reads the first message literally—right-aligns (365,25,3) and (365,3), finds 3 against 3 fine and 25 against 365 fatal—and identifies the cause as a reduction over axis=1 that dropped the axis it needed to broadcast against. Fix: keepdims=True;
  • recognizes the second as and / or / not applied to an array, and gives the replacements &, |, ~ with parentheses, because & binds more tightly than the comparison operators;
  • reads the third as an arithmetic statement about the array, not about the axis argument: the array has two axes, so somewhere upstream a reduction or an integer index already removed one. The fix is to find the missing axis, not to change the 2 to a 1;
  • distinguishes what the message says from what the author meant—the message is always true; the bug is upstream of it.

Listen for this wrong answer: “the arrays are the wrong size, reshape them until it runs.” Reshaping to silence a broadcast error is how a shape bug becomes a numerical bug.

Follow-up probe if the answer is thin: “Which of these three would a unit test on square input have caught, and which would it have hidden?”

Run this first.

First half

B1. Build the study tensor

The BMI study takes measurements on 25 respondents every day for a year, recording three features per respondent: weight (lbs), height (inches), age (years). Fabricate it.

  • weight is uniform on [105, 230]
  • height is uniform on [60, 75]
  • age is uniform on [15, 85]

Store it in a single array T laid out as (day, respondent, feature), with the features in the order above. Use your own seeded generator—np.random.default_rng(6103)—so that your numbers are reproducible.

There is more than one way to fill this array. Whichever you choose, decide before you type what shape each intermediate has.

B2. Predict, then verify

Eight expressions on A = np.arange(24).reshape(2, 3, 4). Write down all eight shapes before you run anything. Put them in predictions as a list of eight tuples, in order.

1.  A.sum(axis=0)
2.  A.sum(axis=(0, 2))
3.  A[:, 1]
4.  A[:, 1:2]
5.  A.mean(axis=2, keepdims=True)
6.  A - A.mean(axis=2, keepdims=True)
7.  A.T
8.  A[..., -1]

A one-element shape is written (3,), with the comma. A scalar has shape ().

Once it passes, run the eight expressions and look at the values for items 3 and 4. They contain the same numbers. Only one of them can be broadcast back against A.

B3. The axis that disappears

From T, compute each of the following. Name the axis you are collapsing before you write the call.

  • daily_feature_means—for each day, the average of the 25 respondents, per feature
  • person_feature_means—for each respondent, the average over the 365 days, per feature
  • feature_means—the average over every day and every respondent, one number per feature
  • heaviest_per_day—for each day, the largest weight recorded that day
  • age_spread—the standard deviation of age over the whole study, a single number
B4. BMI, and centering the right axis

Body mass index from imperial units is \text{BMI} = 703\cdot\frac{\text{weight in lbs}}{(\text{height in inches})^2}.

  1. Compute bmi, one value per respondent per day, shape (365, 25). No loops.
  2. Compute bmi_centred: the same array with each day’s mean subtracted from that day’s readings. Every day gets its own mean. Do it in one expression, with no loop and no np.tile.

The second part is the whole problem. Getting it backwards will still run.

Say in a comment which shape bmi.mean(axis=1) has and why that shape cannot be subtracted from bmi.

Checkpoint—15 minutes

Stop typing. We compare answers to B2 and B4 as a room. Bring the one you got wrong; that is the useful one.

Second half

B5. A function that must not damage its input

Write cap_weights(data, limit). It takes a study tensor laid out like T and returns a new array in which every weight above limit has been replaced by limit. Heights and ages are untouched, and the caller’s array must be exactly as it was.

Now check np.shares_memory(T, capped) yourself, and explain in a comment which line in your function is the one that broke the link.

B6. Rebind or mutate, deliberately

Two functions, same arithmetic, opposite contracts. Standardize means: subtract each column’s mean, then divide by each column’s standard deviation.

  • standardise_copy(x) returns a new standardized array and leaves x untouched.
  • standardise_inplace(x) modifies x in place and returns None.

Write both. The difference between them is one operator, and the self-check can tell.

Then try standardise_inplace(np.arange(12).reshape(4, 3)) in a scratch cell and read the error. One line of comment: why does the copying version survive that input and the in-place version not?

B7. The screening pass

A reading is flagged when its BMI exceeds 30. Using boolean masks and no loops:

  • mask—a boolean array, shape (365, 25), true at flagged readings
  • counts—for each day, how many of the 25 respondents were flagged
  • worst_day—the index of the day with the most flags
  • n_flagged—the total number of flagged readings in the year
  • flagged_weights—the weights of the flagged readings, as a 1D array
  • n_flagged_and_older—how many flagged readings also came from a respondent older than 60

Combine conditions with &, not and, and parenthesise.

np.shares_memory(T, flagged_weights) came back False. Write one comment saying why that was guaranteed before you ran it.

B8. Three tracebacks, three fixes

Each line below raises. The comment states the error and what the author meant. Repair each one—minimally, changing the intent of nothing—and store the results in fix1, fix2, fix3.

For each of the three, add a one-line comment naming the category of the mistake: a broadcasting mismatch, a Python operator used on an array, or an axis that had already been removed.

Where this comes back

None of this is NumPy trivia with a shelf life of one week.

  • Sep 15 (next lab). X - X.mean(axis=0) is the first line of PCA. You wrote its sibling in B4 and again in B8.
  • Regression and ridge. Design matrices are built by stacking and broadcasting, and the difference between a fitted value and a coefficient is a difference of axes.
  • The curse of dimensionality. Pairwise distances are built with exactly the “column against row” broadcasting you used here, at a scale where a needless copy costs you the machine.
  • Dec 8—clustering. Standardizing before you cluster is B6, and doing it along the wrong axis is a mistake that will not raise.
